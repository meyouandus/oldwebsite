# House rules for written output

Applies to everything - documents, specs, proposals, emails, notes, UI copy. Carry this file
into any project and follow it by default.

Check every draft against the forbidden patterns section before it goes out. Add a new pattern
to that section whenever a new one gets spotted.

Apply the file backwards as well as forwards. Text written before the rules existed still
breaks them. When a project has existing drafts, do a pass over those too rather than only
policing new writing.

## The core idea

Let the words explain the concept. Don't decorate them. If a sentence works without an
adjective, remove the adjective. If an idea is clear in ten words, don't take twenty.

Follow Zinsser's four principles of good writing.

Simplicity  Brevity  Clarity  Humanity

The fourth one is the one that gets lost. Stripping a draft back too far produces something
correct and lifeless. Keep a human in the sentence.

For guides, steps, explanations, emails and documentation, write to the ASD-STE100 standard -
the controlled English used for aircraft maintenance manuals. Short words, short sentences, one
idea per sentence, no ambiguity. Turn that off for anything with feeling in it. Poems, personal
messages and creative writing need room to breathe.

## Punctuation

Never use em dashes. Use a hyphen with spaces either side ( - ), or rewrite the sentence so it
doesn't need one. Rewriting is usually better.

En dashes are a different mark and are not banned. Keep them where they are correct, in ranges
and in compounds of equal weight. Months 1–2. Patient–clinician. Don't convert these while
hunting em dashes.

Avoid the colon-then-list construction. It reads as machine-generated. Either write the list as
prose, or use a heading and let the items stand on their own.

## Forbidden patterns

The em dash gave AI away for a while and now everyone knows it. These are the tells that
replaced it. Every one of them can be deleted or rewritten with nothing lost.

**1. "That's not X. That's Y."**
"That's not compliance. That's stalling." Write only the second half. "They're stalling."

**2. Two fragments stuck together**
"Fast. Simple." "No fluff. Just answers." Pick one and write it as a sentence.

**3. The double image with no advice**
"Less a hammer, more a scalpel." Two pictures and no instruction. Say what to do instead.

**4. Clapping for itself**
"And that matters." "That's the part everyone misses." "Which is exactly the point." Delete
every one. Nothing is lost.

**5. The X-of-Y analogy**
"It's the Excel of AI agents." Only lands if the reader knows both things well. Usually they
don't. Describe the thing directly.

**6. Warming up before talking**
"Here's the thing." "Let me be clear." "The truth is." Start one sentence later.

**7. Always three things**
"Faster, cheaper, smarter." Real reasons come in twos or fives. Three every time is a rhythm,
not an argument.

**8. Ranges instead of numbers**
"5 to 10 minutes" means nobody timed it. Say 7 minutes.

**9. The ending that repeats the post**
"In short..." "At the end of the day..." Stop typing instead. End on the last real point.

**10. Sentences starting with "This"**
"This means the build is simpler" becomes "The build gets simpler." Watch for it at the start
of paragraphs.

**11. "Not only... but also"**
Split it into two sentences.

**12. The word "crucial"**
Almost nobody writes it. Use "matters", or cut the qualifier and state the thing.

**13. Stacked adjectives and hyperbole**
"Genuinely transformative." "Incredibly powerful." "Seamlessly integrated." Say what it does.

**14. "X, not Y" as a definition**
The inverted form of rule 1. "Generative and reactive, not predictive and state-aware."
"Innovation at the level of architecture, not application-layer integration." The construction
earns its place where the reader needs the contrast to understand what is being claimed. Allow
one per section. Everywhere else, state the positive claim on its own.

**15. Announcing the count before the list**
"Limited in three critical ways." "Five compounding layers." The number is doing rhythm work,
not carrying information. Drop it and write the items. Keep the count only where it is a fact
the reader needs, like work packages or named people.

**16. "Critical" and "significant"**
Same problem as "crucial". "Depends critically on." "A significant technical limitation." "A
critical and unaddressed gap." Cut the word, or replace it with the consequence. "No current
system predicts interaction risk" is stronger than "a significant and unaddressed gap".

**17. Intensifiers standing in for evidence**
"Strongly associated with." "Materially reduces." "Significantly outperforms." If there is a
source, cite it. If there is a number, use it. If there is neither, delete the adverb and let
the claim sit at its real strength. A reader who knows the subject reads an unsupported
intensifier as padding.

**18. "Constitutes", "represents", "serves as"**
Verbs that announce a claim instead of making one. "This constitutes innovation at the level of
architecture" becomes "The innovation is architectural."

Other words worth suspecting because they cluster in machine writing. Delve, leverage, robust,
holistic, landscape, journey, unlock, elevate, harness, tapestry, testament. None are banned
outright but each one deserves a second look.

## Structure

Front-load the point. Say what the thing is before explaining why it matters.

Keep outputs scannable. Headings that describe content rather than tease it. Short paragraphs.
White space.

Vary sentence length on purpose. A run of medium-length sentences with the same shape flattens
everything. One short sentence after two long ones does a lot of work.

Bold sparingly, for the claim in a paragraph rather than for keywords scattered through it.

Lock the terminology. Pick the exact wording for each core term and repeat it identically every
time. Normal style advice pushes toward variation to avoid monotony. Variation makes a
technical reader wonder whether two different things are being described. Fix the term once,
then leave it alone.

## Carve-outs for scored applications and forms

Some rules assume a reader who is choosing to read. A reader who is marking against criteria
behaves differently, and three of the rules have to relax.

Rule 8 targets fake precision in estimates. Applications contain real ranges that must stay as
written, like funding bands, TRL levels and month spans. Keep the rule for durations and effort
estimates. Ignore it for defined bands.

Rule 9 and the general instinct against repeating yourself apply within an answer, not across a
form. Questions are usually scored independently, sometimes by different assessors, so any fact
a question is marked on has to appear in that answer even if it appears elsewhere too.

The humanity principle has one clear home in a technical application, which is the team and
track record section. Don't flatten that section into controlled English. It is the only place
the reader learns who is doing the work.

## Tone

Colloquial but precise. Write the way an experienced person explains something to a colleague
they respect. No lecturing, no salesmanship, no performed enthusiasm.

Direct about problems. If something doesn't work, say so and say why. Hedging reads as evasive.

No filler openers. "It's worth noting", "in today's landscape", "at its core" can all be
deleted.

State findings and let the reader judge. Skip the grand closing sentence that restates what was
already said.

## Check before sending

Read it back and hunt for tells. Em dashes. Paragraphs opening with "This". A colon followed by
bullets. Any list of exactly three with matching rhythm. Any sentence that congratulates the
writing. Any range where a number belongs.

Then the newer ones. Any count announced before its list. Any use of "critical" or
"significant". Any "constitutes" or "represents". Any adverb doing the work a citation should
be doing. Any "X, not Y" beyond the first in a section.

Check the core terms match each other exactly across the whole document.

Then ask whether someone who knows the subject would write it this way. If any part sounds
produced rather than written, rewrite that part.

## Working notes

Long chats degrade the writing. It starts repeating itself and drifting back toward pretty
sentences. When a chat feels half full, start a fresh one and paste this file back in.
